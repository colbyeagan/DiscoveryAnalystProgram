What a DCF is

A company is worth the cash it will hand to its owners over its lifetime. A DCF forecasts that cash, then shrinks each future amount back to today's money and adds it all up.

Value is future cash

Revenue and earnings are steps along the way. What an owner can actually take out is the cash left after the business pays taxes and reinvests to keep growing. This is called free cash flow.

Time shrinks money

A dollar received in ten years is worth less than a dollar today, because today's dollar could be invested and grow. The discount rate sets how quickly future dollars shrink.

Risk raises the discount rate

Uncertain cash flows are worth less than safe ones. Riskier companies and higher interest rates both push the discount rate up, which pushes the value down.

Discount rate
WACC = (1 − D%) × Ke + D% × Kd × (1 − Tax)  where Ke = Rf + β × ERP and Kd = Rf + Spread
Price per share
Value per share = EV − Net debtShares
FCFF
Free cash flow to the firm: cash left after taxes and reinvestment, before paying lenders or shareholders.
EV (enterprise value)
What the whole business is worth, to lenders and shareholders together.
WACC
Weighted average cost of capital: the blended return that lenders and shareholders require. It is the discount rate.
Terminal value
The value of all cash flows after the forecast ends, treated as a perpetuity that grows at a steady rate.
Net debt
Borrowings minus cash. Lenders are paid first, so it comes off EV to leave the shareholders' part.
Reinvestment
Money put back into plants, equipment and working capital to support growth. It reduces free cash flow.
